Exam Review: Answer Key

Answers to the questions in the Exam Review. If you haven’t tried them yet, go back and do that first - the key is only useful once you know which questions you couldn’t answer on your own.

Utility models & logit

1. A, C, D, and E are true.

  • A) TRUE - \(\beta_2 = 0.2\) is positive, so more capacity adds utility.
  • B) FALSE - \(\beta_4 = -0.5\) is negative, so Leather has lower utility than the reference level, Canvas.
  • C) TRUE - \(\beta_3 = 1.0 > \beta_4 = -0.5\).
  • D) TRUE - a $10 increase changes utility by \(10 \times 0.05 = 0.5\), more than the \(0.2\) from one more liter.
  • E) TRUE - Canvas is the reference level (0), which is higher than Leather’s \(-0.5\).

2.

  • Backpack X (Leather): \(\delta^{\mathrm{Nylon}} = 0\), \(\delta^{\mathrm{Leather}} = 1\)
  • Backpack Y (Canvas, the reference level): \(\delta^{\mathrm{Nylon}} = 0\), \(\delta^{\mathrm{Leather}} = 0\)
  • Backpack Z (Nylon): \(\delta^{\mathrm{Nylon}} = 1\), \(\delta^{\mathrm{Leather}} = 0\)

3.

\[v_X = (-0.05)(40) + (0.2)(20) + (1.0)(0) + (-0.5)(1) = -2 + 4 - 0.5 = 1.5\]

\[v_Y = (-0.05)(60) + (0.2)(30) + (1.0)(0) + (-0.5)(0) = -3 + 6 = 3.0\]

\[v_Z = (-0.05)(50) + (0.2)(25) + (1.0)(1) + (-0.5)(0) = -2.5 + 5 + 1 = 3.5\]

4. Backpack Z, because it has the highest observed utility and logit shares increase with utility. (The actual shares are about 8%, 35%, and 57% for X, Y, and Z.)

5. (a) Discrete: 2 dummy coefficients in either version (one fewer than the number of levels). Continuous: 1 coefficient - but only the ounces version can be continuous, since “Small / Medium / Large” has no numeric value. (b) That every additional ounce adds the same amount of utility (a linear effect), so going from 8 to 12 oz is worth exactly as much as going from 12 to 16 oz.

6. No. The logit probability of choosing A is above 50% but below 100%. The error term \(\varepsilon_j\) captures everything about the choice that we don’t observe, so a person with \(v_A > v_B\) will still sometimes choose B.

Optimization & MLE

7. The log-likelihood is the sum of the log values: \(-3.54 + (-3.35) + (-3.24) + (-3.83) = -13.96\)

8. \(\mu = 52\), because it has the highest (least negative) log-likelihood, \(-13.92\). (Of the values tried, it’s also the one closest to the sample mean of 51.5 hours, which is the maximum likelihood estimate of the mean of a normal distribution.)

9. No. The density values here are all less than 1, and the log of a number less than 1 is negative. Log-likelihoods are usually negative; what matters is which parameter value makes the log-likelihood the highest (least negative).

10. The likelihood is the probability of observing the data we actually observed, treated as a function of the model’s parameters. Maximum likelihood estimation chooses the parameter values that make the observed data most probable.

11. Too large: the search overshoots the optimum and can bounce back and forth or diverge. Too small: it creeps toward the optimum and takes a very long time, or hits the iteration limit before converging.

Uncertainty

12. \(\beta_1\). Sharp curvature means the log-likelihood falls off quickly as you move away from \(\hat\beta_1\), so the data pin it down tightly. Formally, a larger (in magnitude) second derivative in the Hessian means a smaller variance in \(-H^{-1}\), and so a smaller standard error.

13. About \(0.8 \pm 2(0.5) = [-0.2, 1.8]\). The interval contains 0, so the coefficient is not statistically significant at the 95% level.

14. WTP is a ratio of two uncertain - and correlated - estimates, \(\beta / -\alpha\), and ratios are non-linear, so the endpoints don’t divide cleanly. Instead, we take many draws of all the coefficients at once (from a multivariate normal distribution using the covariance matrix from the Hessian), compute the WTP for each draw, and take the 2.5% and 97.5% quantiles of those WTPs.

Design of experiments & power

15. \(4 \times 3 \times 2 = 24\) profiles. Real surveys usually have more attributes and levels, so the full factorial quickly grows into the hundreds or thousands. No respondent can see them all, so we use a fractional design (randomized or D-optimal) that covers the space efficiently with far fewer questions.

16. Standard errors shrink with \(1 / \sqrt{N}\), so cutting the standard error in half takes four times the respondents: about 200.

17. Any two of: more questions per respondent; fewer attributes; fewer levels for categorical attributes; a more efficient (e.g. D-optimal) design.

WTP & market simulation

18. WTP is the attribute coefficient divided by the negative of the price coefficient, \(\omega = \beta / -\beta_1\), with \(-\beta_1 = 0.05\):

  • Nylon: \(1.0 / 0.05 = \$20\)
  • Leather: \(-0.5 / 0.05 = -\$10\)
  • Capacity: \(0.2 / 0.05 = \$4\) per liter

19. It is negative, so on average consumers would need a $10 discount to accept a Leather backpack instead of a Canvas one.

20. \(\hat\lambda = -\hat\beta_1 = 0.05\)

21. The solution with a log-likelihood of \(-1{,}198.2\), the highest of the five. The log-likelihood of a WTP space model is non-convex, so some runs get stuck at local optima. Three runs reaching the same best value is a good sign that it’s the global optimum.

22. Yes. The interval does not contain 0, so the WTP for Leather is statistically significantly negative: consumers do dislike Leather relative to Canvas, by somewhere between about $6 and $14.

23. \(0.02 / 0.4 = 0.05\) thousand dollars, i.e. $50 per mile of range.

24. Share is always highest at the lowest price, but revenue is price × share (× market size). At a very low price you sell a lot but earn little on each unit; at a very high price you earn a lot on each unit but sell very little. Revenue peaks somewhere in between.

25. Any two of:

  • The simulation assumes every consumer knows about and can buy every product (no differences in awareness, availability, or distribution).
  • It only includes the products and attributes in the survey, while real consumers weigh many other things.
  • Survey choices are hypothetical - nobody spends real money.
  • The survey sample may not represent the real market.

Heterogeneity

26. (a) Zero is one standard deviation below the mean, and about 16% of a normal distribution lies more than one standard deviation below its mean, so roughly 16% of people dislike more battery life. (b) How much preferences for battery life vary across the population. A simple logit model estimates one coefficient for everybody.

27. Use a group interaction model when you have a measured variable that you think drives the difference (e.g. student status) and you want to compare those groups directly. Use a mixed logit when preferences vary across people in ways you can’t tie to a variable you measured.

Judging your agent’s work

28. Too many attributes and levels for a respondent to process (fatigue, and people fall back on simple rules like “just pick the cheapest”). It also means many parameters to estimate from very few questions per person, so standard errors will be large and the sample size needed will be huge. Better: fewer attributes and levels, and more questions per respondent.

29. The priors it assumed. A power analysis only tells you what happens if the true coefficients are what you told it they are; if the real effects are smaller, you’ll need more people. Also check that it simulated the same design (attributes, levels, and questions per respondent) that you will actually field.

30. Exactly what counted as “incomplete” (e.g. skipping an optional demographic question shouldn’t throw out someone’s choice data); how many respondents were dropped for each reason; and whether the people who were dropped look different from the ones who were kept, which could bias the results. Ask it to show you the filtering code rather than taking its summary at face value.
